This is an original practice module. Its family example is fictional; it makes no claims about Ian's relatives or a particular historical event.

## The story we inherit {#inherit}

Someone in your family tells you: **“She crossed the country because she never doubted.”**

It is a vivid sentence. It contains an action, a claim about someone's inner life, and a meaning the storyteller wants to preserve. You can appreciate the sentence and still ask what each part rests on.

:::idea[Three things to notice]{#three-things}
**Event:** What is said to have happened?

**Interpretation:** What explanation is being given?

**Meaning:** Why does the story matter to the person telling it?
:::

Keeping these distinct gives you several useful ways into the same story. You might investigate a date, ask where an explanation came from, or simply listen to why a memory is precious.

:::reveal[Look inside that sentence]{#inside-sentence}
“She crossed the country” is a claim about an event. A diary, travel record, or dated letter might help you investigate it.

“Because she never doubted” interprets her motives and experience. It may come from her own words, someone else's recollection, or a later retelling. We do not yet know which.

The story might stand for courage to one person and sacrifice to another. Those meanings tell you something about the speakers, even while the underlying history remains an open question.
:::

## Ask a question that opens a door {#ask}

Try a question that invites another detail:

> What do you remember hearing about how she made that decision?

Then, if the conversation allows it, ask:

> Do you know whether she wrote about it herself?

These are suggested conversation prompts, not a validated interview instrument. Use ordinary language that fits your relationship. A person may choose not to answer.

:::practice[Try it with a story you know]{#try-a-story}

1. Write one sentence you have heard about a relative.
2. Underline the part that describes an event.
3. Circle the part that explains a motive or assigns a meaning.
4. Write one question you could ask without assuming the answer.
   :::

:::reflect[Your next question]{#next-question}
What is one thing you would like to understand better about that story?
:::

## Keep the story and the question {#keep}

Make a small record you can return to:

| Item              | What to write                                                            |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| The story         | The words you remember, clearly marked as a recollection.                |
| Who told it       | The speaker and when you heard it, if known.                             |
| What supports it  | Any document, photograph, recording, or other account you actually have. |
| What remains open | A date, motive, identity, or detail still to investigate.                |
| Why it matters    | Your own response, identified as yours.                                  |

:::idea[Take this with you]{#takeaway}
A useful next step can be small: preserve one story, identify one uncertainty, and ask one generous question.
:::
